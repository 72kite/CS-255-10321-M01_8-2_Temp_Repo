# CS-255-10321-M01_8-2_Temp_Repo
Deliverables from the DriverPass project for CS 255 Systems Analysis. It illustrates translating a startup's need (tackling high DMV test failure rates) into a comprehensive technical plan and system architecture.


Project Summary
The DriverPass Project
DriverPass came to us with a straightforward observation: over 65% of students fail their driving tests because they're studying outdated materials without real preparation. Liam, the owner, and his IT officer Ian wanted to fix that by offering online practice exams tied to current DMV standards, actual on-the-road training, and flexible package options.
The system needed to handle multiple types of users. Customers would book lessons online. A secretary would schedule appointments over the phone. IT staff would manage accounts. Management would review reports. Functionally, it had to manage reservations for two-hour driving sessions, track 10 cars and their drivers, monitor student progress on practice tests, sync with the DMV for updated content, process payments securely, and maintain audit logs showing who did what and when.
Liam's requirements made clear he wanted a cloud based solution accessible from anywhere. He needed reporting that he could download and work with in Excel. The system had to be flexible enough that he could disable training packages without calling a developer. All of this had to happen while keeping customer data safe and integrated with DMV updates.

Reflection on My Work
What Went Well
I approached the interview transcript carefully, picking up on details beyond what was explicitly stated. When Liam said he wanted to access data "anywhere," I recognized that meant cloud hosting, not just having a web interface. This shaped the entire nonfunctional section. I documented 27 functional specifications using the formal "The system shall..." format, which gives developers measurable criteria rather than vague guidance.
The strength of my work was connecting dots between different parts of the system. The user interface section didn't treat all users the same. Each of the four user types got a customized specification of what they needed. A customer needs progress tracking and easy scheduling. A secretary needs rapid data entry tools. An IT officer needs system monitoring and account controls. An owner needs reporting and analytics. Spelling this out in detail prevents building one generic interface that serves nobody particularly well.
The model comparison paper also worked well. Rather than just listing pros and cons, I evaluated which approach actually made sense for DriverPass and recommended a hybrid strategy. That kind of context specific thinking matters more than presenting options neutrally.
What I'd Change
The security section could've been stronger. I hit the basics like encryption, brute force protection, and role based access, but I didn't dig into compliance specifics like GDPR or PCI DSS requirements, data retention policies, or encryption key management. For a system handling payment cards and personal information, that's a gap. I also could've been more specific about audit logging procedures and what happens if security incidents occur.
The limitations section reads too passive. I listed what the system can't do but didn't suggest workarounds or solutions. For instance, I could've proposed offline first synchronization for the limitation around offline data modification. Or recommended a phased approach to DMV integration rather than treating the challenge as fixed. That forward thinking would've made the document more actionable.

Interpreting and Implementing User Needs
I started by treating the interview as a puzzle to solve, not just a checklist to document. When Liam mentioned downloading reports to work on at home using Excel, that wasn't just about file exports. It meant he needed specific formatting, compatibility, and the ability to manipulate data locally. That became a functional requirement for Excel compatible report generation.
My approach was to build separate specifications for each user type rather than assuming one interface could work for everyone. The customer sees progress tracking and scheduling. The secretary sees appointment management and customer lookup. The IT officer sees account administration and system monitoring. The owner sees business analytics. Each spec reflected what that person actually needs to do their job.
Why user needs matter: Systems exist to serve people. When you skip understanding what users actually need, you build systems they'll fight against instead of using. A technically perfect system that doesn't match how people work just frustrates them.
For DriverPass, understanding that Liam wanted flexibility without developer involvement meant the system had to be database driven, not hard coded. Recognizing that a secretary makes phone based reservations shaped the data entry workflow. Knowing that drivers leave notes meant mobile accessibility mattered. These insights only come from really thinking through what users do.
From a business angle, systems built around user needs get adopted more, need less retraining, and actually deliver ROI. Users are productive faster and satisfied with the tool. If DriverPass's system had ignored customer needs for straightforward scheduling and progress visibility, it would fail at the core mission. This isn't theoretical. It directly impacts whether the product succeeds.

How I Approach Software Design
I start with genuine curiosity about the problem space. This means asking detailed questions in interviews, taking notes on both what's said and implied, and checking in with stakeholders to verify I've understood correctly. The time spent up front prevents expensive rework later. It's easier to fix a misunderstanding in the requirements phase than after development.
I break complex problems into pieces, analyzing each while keeping track of how they connect. For DriverPass, I identified the major functional areas like reservations, testing, payments, and user management. Then I examined each piece while considering their interactions. How do reservations affect billing? How does user role affect what reservation data someone can access? This systematic thinking catches gaps.
I design with evolution in mind. Requirements change, and businesses want to add features. Rather than building rigid systems, I look for ways to make things modular and configurable. Cloud platforms, database driven settings, and API first thinking all support this flexibility.
I find value in looking at systems multiple ways. A process model shows me workflows and data movement. An object model shows me entities and relationships. Neither is complete alone. Together they give a fuller picture. Same with use case diagrams, entity relationship diagrams, and sequence diagrams. Each perspective illuminates something different.
Techniques I'll Use Going Forward
Structured requirements capture: I'll keep using formats like "The system shall..." because they eliminate ambiguity. Vague specifications lead to confused implementations and scope creep.
Targeted interview prep: Before talking to clients, I'll prepare specific questions aimed at uncovering what they actually need versus what they think they want. Sometimes those are different.
Early prototyping: I'd like to build rough interface mockups early to show stakeholders what concepts might look like. Catching misalignments before development is much cheaper than after.
Security built in from the start: Not bolted on at the end. I'll use threat modeling and security frameworks from day one, making sure every layer considers security.
Iteration with feedback: Instead of one giant requirements phase, I'll refine designs repeatedly with stakeholder input. Progressive refinement beats trying to get everything right the first time.
Handoff focused documentation: I'll remember that my documents get used by people long after I move on. Developers, testers, maintainers all rely on them. Clarity and organization matter. Removing ambiguity saves them time.
Balancing technical and business reality: The most elegant technical solution sometimes doesn't fit the business constraints. Good design finds the intersection of what's possible and what's practical.

Conclusion
The DriverPass work demonstrates moving from understanding what a client needs to creating detailed specifications that could guide implementation. By focusing on user needs, thinking through problems systematically, and documenting clearly, I created artifacts that bridge the gap between business vision and technical execution. This is the core of systems analysis, understanding problems deeply and translating that understanding into actionable plans.
